When I started building AI systems for **medical coding**, I quickly hit an awkward truth:
I couldn't reason about the AI until I understood the thing the AI was supposed to do. So
I went and learned the basics — and this is my attempt to explain them the way I wish
someone had explained them to me.

This is Part 1: what medical coding is, who does it, and how a single doctor's visit turns
into structured data and a bill. No AI yet — that's Part 2. Think of this as the map before
the territory.

## What medical coding actually is

Every time you see a clinician, they write things down: your symptoms, what they think is
going on, what they did about it. That note is great for a human, but terrible for a
**computer** — "bad ankle sprain, wrapped it, told them to rest" can't be counted,
compared, or paid against a rulebook.

Medical coding is the step that turns that messy free text into **standardized codes**: one
set for *what was wrong* (diagnoses) and another for *what was done* (services and
procedures). Once the encounter is in codes, the same visit can flow through billing,
insurance review, quality reporting, and public-health statistics without anyone re-reading
the chart.

The analogy that finally made it click for me, as someone who builds software: **coding
turns a messy chart into a structured API request.** The clinician's note is unstructured
real-world data; the codes are the typed, validated payload that the payer's system can
actually evaluate against its rules. (If you prefer a non-engineer version: codes are the
**shipping labels** that let a package move through every sorting and tracking system
without anyone opening the box.)

## The code systems (the cast of characters)

There are a few code sets, and the easiest way to keep them straight is by the *question*
each one answers:

| Code set | Answers… | Maintained by | Rough size |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ICD-10-CM** | "What's the diagnosis?" | CDC / NCHS (US version of the WHO's ICD) | ~70,000 codes |
| **CPT** | "What service/procedure was done?" | American Medical Association (AMA) | ~10,000–11,000 codes |
| **HCPCS Level II** | "What supply/item/service *isn't* in CPT?" (e.g., ambulance, equipment) | CMS | Several thousand codes + modifiers |

Two more terms you'll hear, one level up: **DRGs** (Diagnosis-Related Groups) bundle a
hospital inpatient stay into a single payment category instead of paying line-by-line, and
**HCCs** (Hierarchical Condition Categories) group diagnoses to *predict expected cost* for
risk adjustment.

<Callout type="warning" title="">

There isn't just *one* HCC model — Medicare Advantage and the ACA marketplace use different
ones — so it's safer to say "HCCs are diagnosis groupings used to predict cost" than to talk
about "the" HCC system.

</Callout>

If you like the engineering framing: ICD/CPT/HCPCS are the detailed **source files**, and
DRGs/HCCs are **compression layers** that roll those details up into payment or risk buckets.

## Who actually does the coding

Coding sits in a part of the hospital called the **revenue cycle** — the business machinery
that turns care into collected payment. A few roles matter:

- **Medical coders** read the documentation and assign the codes. Crucial boundary: the
  [clinician is legally accountable for *establishing the diagnosis*](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/250974);
  the coder works from what's documented. Garbage in, garbage out.
- **Clinical Documentation Improvement (CDI)** specialists work *upstream*, nudging
  clinicians to document with enough specificity that the codes (and the bill) hold up.
- **Revenue-cycle staff** handle everything downstream: claim edits, submission, payment
  posting, denials, and appeals.

Coding is a credentialed profession, too (the AAPC's **CPC**, AHIMA's **CCA / CCS**, and
others) — part of why "just have an LLM do it" is a bigger claim than it sounds
(foreshadowing Part 2).

A few definitions that tripped me up at first:

- A **claim** is the request for payment your provider sends the insurer after covered care.
- The **payer** is whoever's responsible for paying — a private insurer, or a government
  program like Medicare or Medicaid.
- **Adjudication** is the payer's review-and-decide process.
- An **EOB** (Explanation of Benefits) is the notice *you* get showing what the plan paid
  and what you may owe. Counterintuitively, an **EOB is not a bill** — it just explains the
  decision.
- A **denial** means the payer won't pay some or all of the claim, and you generally have a
  right to appeal (for marketplace plans, an internal appeal is typically due
  [within **180 days**](https://www.healthcare.gov/appeal-insurance-company-decision/appeals/),
  with external review after that).

## A worked example

Let me make it concrete with the example CMS itself uses for teaching.

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example: a sprained ankle">

**Scenario:** an established patient twists their **right ankle**, comes into a clinic, is
examined, and is diagnosed with a sprain (not a fracture).

- **Diagnosis code:** [`S93.401A`](https://www.icd10data.com/ICD10CM/Codes/S00-T88/S90-S99/S93-/S93.401A)
  — "Right ankle sprain, **initial encounter**." That last `A` is a 7th character meaning
  the patient is in *active treatment* (not follow-up). The code also encodes **laterality**
  (right vs. left).
- **Service code:** [`CPT 99213`](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/cpt-code-99213-established-patient-office-visit-20-29-minutes)
  — a common established-patient office visit with low-complexity decision-making (**~20–29
  minutes**); the AMA's own description even lists "**an acute uncomplicated injury**" as a typical
  case.

The provider ties the diagnosis to the service on the claim, the payer adjudicates, and your
share depends on plan rules (deductible, copay, network, allowed amount).

</Callout>

What surprised me is how much the *exact* code depends on documentation: a different side, a
specific torn ligament, a follow-up visit instead of the first one, or added imaging would
each change a code. "Code to the highest specificity the record supports" is a real rule —
and a big reason this is harder than it looks.

## Why it matters (and why it's genuinely hard)

Coding is one of the control points where US healthcare converts care into **money, data,
and accountability**. The stakes are large:

- US national health spending hit about [**$5.3 trillion in 2024**](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet),
  with physician and clinical services around **$1.1 trillion**. Coding decisions operate
  inside *that*.
- **Administrative costs** were about [**7.0% of national health spending in 2024**](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/u-s-spending-healthcare-changed-time/)
  — and note that figure *excludes provider-side* admin, so the real burden of
  billing/coding/claims is almost certainly higher. For comparison, the US spent roughly
  [**$925 per person** on health administration in 2021](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/what-drives-health-spending-in-the-u-s-compared-to-other-countries/)
  vs. **~$245** in comparable countries.
- **Denials are common but the numbers are segment-specific.** In 2024, HealthCare.gov
  marketplace insurers denied about [**19% of in-network claims**](https://www.kff.org/patient-consumer-protections/claims-denials-and-appeals-in-aca-marketplace-plans-in-2024/)
  (~20% of all claims), and fewer than **1%** of denials were appealed.
- **Coding errors are a major compliance risk.** CMS found incorrect coding drove about
  [**49%** of improper payments for evaluation-and-management codes](https://www.cms.gov/training-education/medicare-learning-networkr-mln/compliance/medicare-provider-compliance-tips/evaluation-management-services)
  in a recent period, and estimated the overall Medicare fee-for-service improper-payment
  rate at **6.55%** (~$28.8B) for FY2025. 
- [**Upcoding**](https://oig.hhs.gov/compliance/physician-education/i-physician-relationships-with-payers/)
  — billing for something more severe or expensive than was provided — can cross into fraud,
  though a lot of "improper payments" are really just *insufficient documentation*, which
  isn't the same as fraud. That distinction matters.

So a "good code" quietly does a lot of work, and a "bad code" can mean a wrong payment, a
denial, a compliance problem, or bad downstream data. That's the gap AI is being asked to
help with.

## A quick Boston detour

Since I'm in Boston, a few Massachusetts specifics make this concrete: the state runs its
own marketplace (the [**Massachusetts Health Connector**](https://www.mass.gov/connector)),
still has an individual coverage mandate, calls its Medicaid program
[**MassHealth**](https://www.mass.gov/topics/masshealth), and offers an external-appeal path
through the state's [**Office of Patient Protection**](https://www.mass.gov/request-an-external-review-of-a-health-insurance-decision).
Massachusetts even maintains an [**All-Payer Claims Database**](https://www.mass.gov/chia) —
a reminder that all those codes become a public dataset used to study the whole system, not
just to pay one bill.
